---
title: SimulationMaxxing: How Nubank Ships Agents 20× Faster
speaker: Aman Gupta, Nubank & Shreya Rajpal, Snowglobe
video: https://youtu.be/KMR_RBoCa4M
---
Nubank is Latin America's largest digital bank, serving 135 million customers across Brazil, Mexico, and Colombia, with quarterly revenue past $5 billion. Its customer support model pairs human agents with AI agents, and the talk opens with proof this pairing works: TNPS (a customer-satisfaction metric) for five production AI agents has climbed steadily over several quarters, with many agents now approaching or exceeding human-level quality. The claimed mechanism behind these gains is simple to state: generating eval data in simulation instead of waiting on production data lets teams ship agents roughly 20x faster.

The case for this starts from why evals are the real bottleneck in agent development. Evals reduce to two ingredients — metrics and data. Metrics are the more tractable half; teams have a workable playbook using LLM-as-judge classifiers aligned to human judgment and refined via auto-prompt optimization. Data is the hard part, and it's gotten harder with agents. Where earlier ML and single-turn chatbot data could be treated as structured rows, a single agent data point is now a full multi-turn trajectory with internal tool calls and state that must stay consistent throughout — expensive to generate and even more expensive to scale into a representative sample set.

Teams typically source eval data two ways, and both are painful. Manual authoring is extremely slow: someone has to hand-plan trajectories and state updates and keep them consistent across every tool call. Production traces are nearly free to obtain but come at the cost of testing on real, live users, which makes it hard to run many parallel experiments safely. In practice, changing an agent's harness, prompts, or tools might take a few hours, but running offline evals on hand-curated data can take days, and confirming a real improvement via a production A/B test — waiting on sparse, noisy customer feedback — can take weeks or longer.

Simulation, provided by Snowglobe, is proposed to collapse this timeline to hours or minutes. Mechanically, it works by wrapping the target agent via an SDK with no code changes, identifying which tools need to be mocked, and then defining how to steer the simulation — which personas, use cases, and scenarios to generate. The output is a dataset of thousands of multi-turn conversations against the real agent, complete with mocked tools and consistent synthetic state, which then feeds directly into an evaluation pipeline with per-turn judge scores. A concrete example: a synthetic persona (a 34-year-old designer applying for her first credit card) is generated with consistent grounding data — a fake address, fake credit card details, a tone and interaction style — and then run through the agent to produce a full conversation faithful to that persona's intent, with tool calls and account data staying consistent throughout.

This powers a self-improvement loop: ship an agent, observe it, build robust evals (often via automated prompt optimization techniques like DSPy-style algorithms), run simulation, pipe both simulated and real data through the evals for rich signal, optimize the harness, verify, and ship again — enabling many more A/B tests per agent than before. To trust this loop, the team validated that simulated data tracks production data well: correlation between sim-based and real eval scores was high, and human domain-expert review confirmed roughly 80% of sim-based labels were usable, for both mature and greenfield agents.

Concrete wins followed. Simulation caught a regression before it reached production and flagged an issue that would have hurt self-service rate. One agent's TNPS doubled after adopting simulation-driven, principled iteration instead of trial and error. Teams now iterate against simulation until satisfied before launching just one A/B test, rather than burning through five or six. Simulation also let Nubank improve self-service rate by up to 4% without trading it off against satisfaction — a tension often assumed in customer-service settings — and let the team rapidly benchmark newly released open-source models against their agent harness, saving what would otherwise be multiple weeks of evaluation work per model.

The closing takeaways: generating eval data in simulation rather than relying solely on production breaks the release-speed bottleneck; closing the sim-to-real gap via offline/online/human-review comparisons is essential to trust simulated results; and ultimately, building a genuinely self-improving agent comes down to having aligned metrics and a reliable way to generate data those metrics can score — the combination that makes a continuous improvement loop possible.
