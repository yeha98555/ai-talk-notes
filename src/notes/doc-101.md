---
title: Every Question Has a Price: Rebuilding the Data Stack for Agents
speaker: Ethan Ding, TextQL
video: https://youtu.be/PwhlxJrjQgQ
---
The starting thesis is that AI collapses the cost of moving data and business logic between systems to nearly zero, which undercuts the entire premise of the modern data stack: if you no longer need to migrate everything into a warehouse or lake to make it usable, you can instead connect to every source system exactly where it lives and make it interoperable from day one. This idea came out of watching enterprises burn years moving the same business logic from Redshift to Snowflake to Databricks, and from a co-founder's high-frequency-trading background, where the firms that win are the ones that spend the least time cleaning data and the most time turning information into an executed decision.

The modern data stack is described as a 1970s assembly line: every stage — ETL into a warehouse, data modeling, semantic-layer curation, and finally dashboard consumption — requires a human touch, and each handoff adds latency. TextQL's agent, Anna, is built to work directly across ERPs, CRMs, EHRs, warehouses, databases, and BI tools rather than being stapled to one platform; it can build a dashboard in Tableau, push a calculated field into dbt, and update a warehouse ingestion pipeline as the same agent, on-prem if needed, stitching together hundreds of databases across multiple clouds within an afternoon. Because dashboards can now be generated on demand in about 30 seconds, the argument goes, most of them should become throwaway, just-in-time artifacts rather than long-lived, heavily governed objects maintained for months to justify the labor that built them.

Messy data is reframed as a solved problem, not a blocker. Rather than routing every new metric through a governance committee that takes three weeks to approve a definition, TextQL lets business users define metrics locally and "remember this for next time," which generates a pull request against the GitHub repo holding the semantic layer; data teams then review proposals in bulk. At the volumes agents produce — tens of thousands of data requests a month — governance necessarily shifts from auditing every metric to directional accuracy: parsing which queries and tables recur most often and canonizing those as golden metrics, which is argued to be more sustainable than committee-driven definitions that get bypassed anyway when business users just export to Excel.

Analytics accuracy is framed through an expected-value, trading-like lens rather than a search for perfect precision. The design target for a decision loop is spending a few dollars of tokens and compute to produce an outcome worth an order of magnitude more in expected value, sampling across data sources to raise confidence rather than demanding certainty — the claims-fraud example given is denying a $100,000 claim at 95% confidence and being fine with the 5% error, because the cost of querying further outweighs the marginal accuracy gained. Some domains, like closing the books in FP&A, still demand near-total accuracy, but many enterprise decisions only need to beat the status quo by a couple of percentage points to unlock outsized P&L impact, especially in large financial-services and insurance environments.

On lock-in, TextQL deliberately avoids monetizing storage or seats, the model most vendors (Tableau, Databricks) rely on to keep customers bound to their platform. Context, memory, and semantic-layer YAML are stored in the customer's own GitHub repo as their IP, and the pitch is that the vendor only makes money by expanding what customers can query — meaning it has to stay the best option continuously rather than relying on switching costs. This is also offered as the answer to why turning a general coding agent loose on a warehouse isn't sufficient long-term: it works well at first, but breaks down on role/column-level RBAC across many databases, leaves no persistent ownership of the semantic layer, and — most acutely — triggers runaway cost as business users escalate from asking "what is this number" to "why" to "tell me what to do next," often turning single queries into hourly cron jobs. TextQL's optimization instead aims to make each subsequent query cheaper by learning from prior ones — collaborative-filtering-style prioritization of frequently hit tables, and effectively materializing expensive, frequently-aggregated views — so median per-query cost falls over time rather than rising with usage.

The advice to data leaders is to own their own storage, business logic, and compute engine rather than renting infrastructure bound to a single warehouse vendor, and to make the whole stack legible to agents by putting it in an open, file-based form: dashboards as folders of SQL and JavaScript, semantic layers as headless files outside the warehouse, and schemas in open table formats like Iceberg queried by headless engines. The urgency is that warehouse query volume is about to explode — TextQL already accounts for roughly 75% of net-new SQL at some customers — and warehouse bills, as a complement to LLM token spend, are about to spike in ways companies aren't budgeting for.

Asked to bet on the next 18 months, the prediction is that most enterprise SQL will be AI-written, and most of that will come from business users prompting AI in "questionable ways" that require active shaping and governance. Citizen-data-scientist queries, currently under 10% of total warehouse query volume, could rise to 70–90%, straining data-warehouse architectures that weren't built for that scale and demanding new approaches to replication and cost management.
