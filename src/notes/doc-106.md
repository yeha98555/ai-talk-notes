---
title: Video Has No Memory. Here's How We Built One.
speaker: James Le, TwelveLabs
video: https://youtu.be/mOf-PP4mVjA
---
The talk opens with a provocation: despite video being a recording of the past, most video-AI systems have no memory in the systems sense. The core argument is that video should not be treated as a bag of frames — a stack of images plus maybe a transcript — because that discards the continuity that makes video meaningful. A better mental model is a spatial-temporal volume containing visual content, speech, sound, motion, OCR, camera changes, scene transitions, metadata, and time, and the hard problem is preserving relationships across that volume so applications can traverse millions of moments across petabytes of footage.

Forcing video into a text-first stack creates three failure modes. Wrong context: sampling frames or extracting transcripts and dumping them into a prompt destroys the spatial-temporal relationships that actually define events. Wrong memory: text-system notions of memory (retrieval, vector search, larger context windows) don't capture the durable continuity video needs — linking today's scene to something in another file, episode, camera angle, season, or year. Weak reasoning: text-first systems can't natively reason over motion and causality, so they never build a persistent structure of who appeared, what happened, and what changed.

Five properties of video explain why this is hard: it's temporal (meaning depends on before/after, so a single frame can mislead), multimodal (a transcript alone misses the logo, a frame alone misses the spoken claim), dense (a few minutes contains dozens of shots and claims with uneven signal), ambiguous (people and places recur under different lighting, angles, or partial visibility, and concepts accrue meaning over time rather than in one moment), and expensive to verify (enterprise workflows need to trace claims back to the exact source moment).

TwelveLabs' stack addresses this in layers: semantic chunks capture meaningful temporal units; a multimodal embedding encoder (Marengo) turns those spans into spatial-temporal vector embeddings; a spatial-temporal context store preserves reusable structures like moments, entities, and metadata; and a video-context-aware language model (Pegasus) serves as the reasoning layer for summaries, metadata synthesis, and comparison — all exposed as APIs.

A key distinction is search versus memory. Search recovers relevant moments from a large library but returns candidates without continuity. Memory preserves entities, timelines, and evidence across an entire corpus, enabling a shift from "show me something like this" to "tell me what this collection knows" — returning structured knowledge and timelines instead of a single clip. This memory needs to scale along two axes: time (reasoning over years of footage without reprocessing the archive, via reusable representations that support multi-hop, episodic recall at low latency) and space (fusing evidence across multiple camera angles, streams, or body cams into one current understanding).

The proposed representation is a context graph: a durable, queryable structure connecting time-bounded moments (evidence units) at the base, appearances (where and when an entity shows up) above that, then entities (people, brands, places, concepts), relationships (co-occurrences, sequences across time and place), and corpus-level context (themes, patterns, coverage gaps) at the top. Different questions traverse different parts of the graph — simple search hits moments directly, entity workflows expand from a person into appearances, and narrative questions follow relationships across time.

Five design principles follow: ingest once and reason many times, paying the interpretation cost upfront rather than per query; store primitives (moments, entities, appearances) rather than just answers, so they support downstream search, editing, and analytics; ground every claim to a specific timestamp in the source video; let intent shape memory, since the same footage serves different primitives for brand safety, compliance, or creator analytics, so the layer must be configurable; and keep the layer composable and API-first so applications can plug in structured, graded metadata.

Building the "harness" around a video worker (versus a static, stateless model that produces one answer) means the system should support memory, task planning (search vs. summarization vs. multi-step reasoning), retrieval of the right evidence, expert tools (zoom, frame comparison, metadata enrichment), an operating envelope (explicit limits on time, cost, scope, autonomy), an output contract (natural language vs. structured data with references and timestamps), and evaluation of whether retrieval, synthesis, and budget adherence were correct.

Three demos illustrate the approach using TwelveLabs' agent product, Jockey: sports understanding on 67 World Cup videos (finding near-misses and explaining why they weren't goals, describing goal build-ups with passer/scorer detail, and tracking a single player like Messi across an entire corpus with camera-framing description); security/surveillance on public camera footage (counting and classifying vehicles and pedestrians at an intersection, detecting safety events like near-collisions and red-light violations, and handling varied conditions like rain and crowded scenes in Bangkok); and advertising analysis on a five-minute Adidas World Cup commercial (classifying moments suitable for ad placement — reveals, hard cuts, impact/energy beats, logo appearances — useful for advertisers choosing where to fit branded content).

The closing framing positions this as video cognition infrastructure rather than an application layer: a knowledge store as the memory layer, configurable ingestion to shape what gets extracted, corpus digest for library-level understanding, and a resolution/search/response API — intended for workflows spanning media archives, content creators, sports analysis, and advertising, currently in private beta.
