---
title: From Systems of Record to Systems of Context
speaker: Omri Bruchim & Tomer Ast, monday.com
video: https://youtu.be/Btk8wDUVs74
---
The talk argues that the shift AI assistants need isn't from a system of record to more retrieval — it's from record-keeping to genuine understanding, and it uses monday.com's own agent, Sidekick, to illustrate what that means in practice.

The starting example is a deceptively simple question everyone asks each morning: what should I focus on right now? Point any current assistant — Gemini, GPT, Claude — at this question and, even with full access to your boards, tasks, emails, and Slack, you get a list of disconnected bullet points dressed up as a confident paragraph. The assistant has all the data but zero understanding of how it connects. That gap, the speakers argue, is the real problem — not missing data or weak retrieval, but missing understanding of how entities relate to each other and to the user's actual priorities.

Three reasons make this hard. First, the "agent gap": an agent is sharp at executing a well-specified task (draft a reply to this escalation) but has no way to infer what the priority even is, no matter how much memory it holds. Second, records don't carry meaning — a status change or log entry never explains why it happened; understanding requires tracing context backward, the way a developer might follow a line of code to its commit, to its PR description, to the customer complaint that triggered it in the first place. Third, this kind of meaning can't be assembled at query time — it's too late to reconstruct the full causal chain the moment a question is asked, so understanding has to be built ahead of time, continuously, not synthesized on demand.

This motivates what the team calls the Monday World Model: a persistent, pre-built context layer that follows a user's work rather than a bigger prompt or longer context window. It's constructed from thousands of signals — item status changes, activity logs, messages, meetings — organized into three layers: the structure of a user's work (entities and their relationships, what depends on what, how a Slack message connects to a task), a live snapshot (what's overdue, what's urgent right now, who you're actively collaborating with), and a durable profile learned over time (decisions, outcomes, work rhythms).

This model is built using two engines running on different cadences: a slow engine that mines weeks of activity to infer persona, routines, collaborators, and ongoing goals into a reinforced, durable profile, and a fast engine that recomputes live signals — what's suddenly urgent, who you've just been pulled into a thread with — over a short recent window. One engine learns who you are; the other tracks your day. The speakers note this split mirrors two established ideas from other fields: complementary learning systems in neuroscience (hippocampus for instant capture, neocortex for distilled long-term lessons) and lambda architecture in data engineering (a fast speed layer merged with a slow batch layer into one served view) — two unrelated domains converging on the same pattern.

Operationally, data is ingested continuously from every surface a user works in — monday.com, Slack, email, calendar — and both engines precompute their layers offline, ahead of time. When a user engages the agent, only a thin slice of recent-activity logic is recomputed live, and the full context is served for the agent to reason over and traverse as needed. This architecture yields two properties by design: resilience, since sources are isolated so one bad feed doesn't break the whole model and the system degrades gracefully by falling back to last-verified context rather than failing outright; and calibrated urgency, letting the agent judge when to proactively notify a user versus stay silent. Critically, the model compounds — each day of captured data sharpens the profile, and adding a new source is cheap and only additive, so the system's understanding strictly grows and becomes uniquely tailored to each user.

The speakers are candid about limits: the model always trails the live world, new users start with too little reliable data to reason from, and every signal carries its own bias, making noise-versus-signal discrimination the hardest ongoing problem. Even so, walking back through the opening question, they show Sidekick now answering it concretely — surfacing pending commitments, an unanswered VP email, and a relevant Slack aside the user didn't repeat in standup — by drawing on the profile (who you are, what you're working on) and the live signals (what's due, what's said) built ahead of time. Their conclusion: the bottleneck was never data access but the connective understanding between data points, which even the most capable underlying model can't supply on its own — it has to be engineered beforehand.
