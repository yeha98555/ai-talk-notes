---
title: Agents at Scale: Inside MiniMax's Model and the Infrastructure Behind It
speaker: Olive Song, MiniMax & Dan, Together AI
video: https://youtu.be/AVMr9PMINyo
---
This panel pairs a model creator with an inference provider to walk through what it actually takes to train and serve a frontier open-weight model at scale, using MiniMax M3 and its Together AI deployment as the running example.

MiniMax open-sources its models because it believes a strong open-source community compounds: releasing weights lets outside developers optimize, contribute fixes, and push the model further, aligning with a mission of making intelligence broadly available. The MiniMax–Together partnership grew organically from following each other's work across earlier MiniMax releases (2.5, 2.7) before M3, to the point where Together now carries the largest share of M3 token usage.

M3's biggest departure from the M2 series is that it's multimodal from scratch — trained jointly on text and image data from step zero rather than bolted on later. Naive multimodal training often causes models to collapse partway through; MiniMax solved this, and found that models trained this way develop attention maps where text tokens naturally attend to visual tokens, which pays off directly in tasks like agentic web-app development and computer-use, where the model must both see and act on a UI. Hidden-gem use cases the team highlighted include computer-use navigation and game development, alongside the benchmarks called out at launch — SVG-bench, kernel-bench, and OS-world.

Post-training for these specialized domains is fundamentally an environment-and-data design problem: for kernel optimization, it means building complex environments where reinforcement learning can iteratively improve GPU kernel performance, and formulating rewards carefully so the model actually improves rather than reward-hacks. Together AI's own contribution here is a benchmark called Parallel Kernel Bench, deliberately built from open, unsolved kernel problems; unlike benchmarks people worry about overfitting to, this one is built so that even if a model overfits it, the kernels it produces are genuinely useful and can be adopted straight into the inference stack.

On the serving side, the moment an inference team gets early architectural details of a new model — the attention variant, the MoE routing, the quantization choices, all of which differ meaningfully across open models — kernel writing and optimization begin. Together's launch-day goal is only quality parity and a usable experience; over the following weeks a running list of optimizations (KV-cache handling, attention kernels, quantization work) lands incrementally, so the same model is noticeably faster at day seven and day fourteen than at day one. The shift in workload from chat-shaped (short system prompt, a few conversational turns) to agent-shaped (an entire codebase uploaded, hundreds of tool calls, long multi-turn sessions) has also changed which parts of the stack are worth optimizing — prompt handling, KV-cache strategy, and routing all have to serve long, code-heavy contexts rather than short chat turns.

M3 raises inference complexity further with a custom sparse-attention design (distinct from DeepSeek's or GLM's approaches), multimodality, and context extended to a million tokens. There is no single highest-leverage optimization — the practice is to optimize nearly everything, kernel by kernel, while building on sparse-attention research the Together team had invested in well before the topic went mainstream, even though each new sparse-attention design still needs bespoke kernels.

One training result singled out as remarkable is the model's ability to reproduce a 12-hour research task — replicating an ICLR paper end to end. Training for long-horizon, hardware-constrained tasks like this again comes down to environment and reward design, plus adaptations to the RL algorithm for efficiency. Evaluation runs throughout the rollout rather than only at the end: intermediate submissions are checked against internal evals (some of them built through MiniMax using its own models to accelerate its development process — the self-evolution loop) to catch reward hacking and confirm the model is genuinely improving rather than producing a plausible-looking final answer.

Managing KV cache at this scale — million-token contexts crossed with heavy concurrent traffic — is described as effectively building a distributed file system or large database: deciding where cache lives, detecting when it can be reused, and moving it efficiently between machines is conceptually simple and brutally hard in practice.

Asked what will look primitive three years from now, both speakers point in the same direction: industry-wide GPU utilization during training is still low and should improve dramatically, and the gap between closed frontier labs and open models — already smaller than commonly believed, as M3, GLM, and Kimi show — should keep shrinking. On the training side, the compounding effect of models increasingly building and accelerating their own development pipelines (self-evolution) is picked as the trend most likely to keep progress accelerating.
