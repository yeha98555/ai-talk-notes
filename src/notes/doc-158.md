---
title: Data and Environment Curation for Post-Training LLMs
speaker: Mahesh Sathiamoorthy, Bespoke Labs
video: https://youtu.be/ewtOo0scUh0
---
The talk argues that as AI shifts from being evaluated on what models know to what agents can do, the main obstacle to long-horizon agent autonomy is reliability, and post-training — SFT and RL — remains one of the most powerful levers for improving it. Yet for both enterprises and frontier labs, compute and training infrastructure are now well understood while good data and RL environments remain the real bottleneck; RL environments are simply framed as data in a different shape.

Bespoke Labs' philosophy is to sit at the intersection of data/environment creators and the researchers who consume that data, curating with the researcher's incentives in mind — what actually moves benchmark metrics — rather than curating in the abstract. This grew out of building Curator, a tool for synthetic SFT data curation, and expanded into Open Thoughts, a consortium with Stanford, UC Berkeley, and University of Washington formed after DeepSeek's release exposed a shortage of high-quality open reasoning data. The Open Thoughts pipeline starts from a pool of source questions drawn from many datasets, mixes and filters them with LLM-based judges for quality and difficulty, generates answers using teacher reasoning models (DeepSeek, Qwen-based models, Gemini, etc.), and optionally filters or resamples answers — with every stage tuned via ablations to find a curation recipe that scales: more curated data reliably improves benchmark performance. Counterintuitive findings included that sampling multiple answers per question (rather than answering more unique questions once) improved results, likely due to reasoning-trace diversity, and that stronger teacher models were not always better data generators.

The same recipe was extended into Open Thoughts Agents, curating data and RL environments for training agents rather than reasoning models. Sources such as Stack Exchange were mixed and filtered similarly, rollouts were generated and a teacher model chosen, and again weaker models sometimes outperformed stronger ones as teachers, and sampling multiple rollouts helped while synthetic rewriting and task augmentation did not. Notably, SFT alone contributed most of the performance gains, with RL — despite being far more compute-intensive — adding only marginal improvement at the end, suggesting SFT is often sufficient and practical for enterprise settings.

A concrete production example came from an engagement with Intuit's Credit Karma, where a model explains why a given credit card was recommended. Prompting alone struggled with compliance, since a long list of regulatory rules had to be enforced and baking all of them into the prompt blew up latency. Post-training on curated data seemed straightforward, but the underlying data was highly imbalanced (e.g., many 0% APR cases), causing the fine-tuned model to hallucinate specific numbers. The fix was to restructure training examples with explicit tags that pushed the model to focus on the correct form of the response rather than memorizing specific figures. This improved compliance, latency, and throughput, and let the company own a smaller model rather than depend on increasingly expensive frontier APIs.

The talk closes with a reference architecture for the emerging RL/post-training stack: a top layer for post-training methods (SFT, RL, and prompt optimization techniques like Gepa, which use LLM reflection to iteratively improve prompts and harnesses), a middle layer for building, measuring, and versioning RL environments, and a bottom layer of infrastructure for sandboxes, rollout generation, and checkpointing/rollback for long-horizon rollouts — a stack the speaker sees converging across multiple teams building toward reliable, autonomous agents.
