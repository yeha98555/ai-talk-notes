This is the second livestream in a series on training agents, following an earlier session on supervised fine-tuning (SFT) over agent traces. The frame for the whole series is post-training: pre-training gives a model general language ability and world knowledge, mid-training adds domain knowledge like code, and post-training (SFT, distillation, RL) teaches the targeted behaviors and output format. Distillation is presented as the workhorse for making small, cheap, fast models good at a specific task by learning from a larger or domain-expert model — useful for compression, capability transfer, and synthetic data generation. The talk is careful to frame distillation as a decades-old, standard ML technique (Hinton 2015, sometimes called model extraction), not the adversarial "distillation attack" framing that has become popular rhetoric.

Distillation is organized along four axes: signal (training on the teacher's output text — hard labels — versus its full logit distribution — soft labels, which carries richer information about which tokens were plausible runner-ups); data source (white-box access to logits and generation process versus black-box access to only text); timing (offline, where teacher data is generated before training starts, versus online, where the teacher is in the training loop); and policy (off-policy, training on the teacher's own trajectories, versus on-policy, training on trajectories the student itself generates, graded by the teacher). A chess analogy motivates why on-policy matters: RL gives a sparse reward only at the end of a game, so a model that can't even make a legal first move never gets signal; off-policy distillation (like SFT on teacher traces) is denser but risks irrelevance if the teacher (a grandmaster) never faces the situations the student actually struggles with; on-policy distillation solves both problems by grading the student's own attempts, giving dense and relevant feedback.

In TRL, a single class — the (experimental) Generalized Knowledge Distillation (GKD) Trainer — implements both regimes via two knobs: lambda controls where training data comes from (0 = off-policy, learn from fixed teacher data; 1 = on-policy, learn from student-generated data), and beta controls the KL direction (0 = forward KL over the teacher's whole distribution; 1 = reverse KL focused on the teacher's top choices). For off-policy, the demo reproduced the prior session's coding-agent experiment but swapped SFT for sequence/logit knowledge distillation: a coding agent was instructed to fine-tune a 0.6B student against a 4B teacher on the same Pi coding-trace dataset, sweeping learning rates via HF Jobs, with all scripts, models, and Trackio dashboards published together as a Hub collection.

On-policy distillation traces to the DAGGER paper and is used in recent frontier releases (GLM-5.2, Qwen3, DeepSeek). Its loop: sample a completion from the student, run a single cheap prefill pass of those tokens through the frozen teacher to get its per-position distribution, compute a per-token reverse-KL loss between student and teacher, and update the student. Reverse KL is mode-seeking rather than mean-seeking (forward KL): instead of pulling the student toward an average of the teacher's distribution — which may not correspond to anything the teacher actually did — it pushes the student toward the specific high-probability modes that best represent the teacher's actual behavior. In TRL this is the same GKD Trainer with lambda=1 and beta=1; intermediate values can blend in some off-policy exposure or soften the mode-seeking behavior, though 0/1 is recommended as the starting point for learning the algorithm. The same experimental setup (0.6B student, 4B teacher, Pi coding traces, three learning rates) was rerun on-policy for comparison via Trackio logs.

A closing section covers self-distillation, where the "teacher" is an earlier or privileged version of the same model, with three TRL-supported variants. Simple self-distillation (SDFT-style) restores a capability lost during a later training phase — e.g., recovering instruction-following after a code-focused fine-tune degraded it — by distilling from an earlier checkpoint that still had that capability, making checkpoint management central to a training pipeline. SDPO (self-distillation policy optimization) generates two traces for the same task, where the second is given privileged information or environment feedback (e.g., a solution found after a long search, or tool/environment feedback) so the model learns to reach a good outcome faster, with the loss computed as before between the two traces. SDFT (self-distilled fine-tuning) trains on the best runs within a sampled group. The line between this and RL (e.g., process-reward methods) is genuinely blurry, and real training pipelines — Nemotron 3's post-training section is cited as a deep example — mix distillation and RL techniques across many domain-specialist checkpoints to assemble a final model.

The fundamental ceiling for on-policy/off-policy distillation is the teacher's own performance, since the student is being pulled toward it; self-distillation can exceed prior checkpoints because it reuses privileged information or earlier capabilities rather than an external teacher's ceiling. RL's ceiling instead depends on the environment and reward design, motivating the next session in the series. Audience Q&A covered multi-teacher on-policy distillation (not natively supported in TRL but buildable as an extension, and already used by some frontier labs), the impossibility of on-policy/logit-based distillation from closed, API-only teacher models (limited to offline text-based distillation, or separate synthetic-data-generation-plus-judge pipelines like those behind Deita or WizardLM), and the broader point that production pipelines freely mix checkpoints and algorithms — distillation, SFT, RL — rather than following one fixed recipe.
