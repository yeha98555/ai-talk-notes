---
title: Training Frontier Models to Out-Think Hackers
speaker: Uri Rolls, Arithmetic & Thom Wolf, Hugging Face
video: https://youtu.be/O-CBZ3JtRvo
---
The talk opens with a claim that cybersecurity is a far richer playing field for frontier AI than most people assume, comparable in spirit to ARC-AGI-3: the new benchmark being introduced asks models to build and act on a dynamic model of a system they've never seen, and current frontier models solve only one to two percent of it, because they still can't reliably infer how clicking one thing changes the state of another. The speakers frame this as evidence that raw intelligence isn't the bottleneck — world-modeling and multi-step logical inference are.

The motivating worry is that the economics of cyber are inverting. Historically, defense had the harder job — block every door and window while an attacker only needs to find one crack — but that asymmetry assumed attackers had to choose targets carefully. A sufficiently capable model, open or closed, now lets a single skilled attacker probe many targets simultaneously, and defensive systems, which must run at scale with minimal human intervention, are bounded by what off-the-shelf models can do. The proposed answer, drawing an explicit analogy to how AI transformed coding over the past year, is to make models themselves radically better at cyber defense through high-quality evals, data, and benchmarks, so defenders eventually outpace attackers.

That's the purpose of Masov, Arithmetic's new benchmark, which deliberately narrows scope to access control rather than trying to cover all of cyber at once. Access control is chosen because it's the perennial number one issue on the OWASP list, underlies a roughly $30 billion remediation industry, and — crucially — its vulnerabilities are logic-based rather than simple code bugs: large systems with independent components that check permissions differently create seams where privilege escalation becomes possible. Exploiting these requires exactly the kind of holistic, iterative world-modeling the ARC-AGI comparison invokes.

The benchmark's environments are built by human vulnerability researchers who find real zero-day exploits in widely used open-source software, then chain multiple real applications (e.g., Keycloak, Vault, a message broker) into black-box settings where the model has no access to source code or the internet, only standard tooling. Because every task is a real, previously unknown exploit chain, every step toward exploitation can be deterministically graded, letting the team measure not just pass/fail but how deep into the chain a model gets — mirroring an input/agent/verifiable-grader eval structure credited to Anthropic's Eugene Cheah.

A live trace illustrates the difficulty: starting as a low-privileged user, the correct solve requires noticing that one permission check validates admin status by name while another checks by ID, so renaming yourself to match the admin lets you inherit their privileges. Watching GPT-5.5 and Opus attempt this, both probe chaotically, reach the relevant check, and complete discovery of the environment, but never make the specific logical leap to renaming themselves for escalation. Across the current benchmark leaderboard, only GPT-class models solve any task at all, with just one success at k=1, underscoring how far even frontier models are from robust logic-chain reasoning in live systems.

The closing argument is that this capability gap is exactly why open, fine-tunable models matter: cyber defense will ultimately be a speed contest between attacker and defender once systems are breached, favoring specialized models running on specialized, fast hardware rather than dependence on any one or two closed providers. The team frames their roadmap as building good data and benchmarks first, then post-training models per environment and network, and is inviting outside collaborators — especially vulnerability researchers interested in domains of cyber beyond access control — to help generate the post-training data needed to get there.
