The talk opens with a positioning question: in a world where frontier models and coding agents make it trivial to spin up new AI capability fast, what's actually defensible once you push to production? The claim is that the durable moat isn't the chat interface, the agent framework, or even the model — all of that is commodity and will keep getting replaced by better SaaS products and new model releases. What's genuinely defensible is an organization's deep, tacit understanding of its own internal processes: how data is actually joined, what fields mean, what reporting conventions and safeguards apply, and how questions have historically been answered. That tacit knowledge, encoded into a structured, agent-consumable model, is what the Gates Foundation set out to build as its Strategic Intelligence Platform (SIP), rolled out enterprise-wide this past month to roughly 4,000 staff.

The scale of the underlying operation motivates the effort: over 25 years the Foundation has produced enormous, siloed structure — thousands of grants a year, many over $5 million, spread across 100+ countries and multiple divisions (global health, global development, gender equality, and others), with over $7 billion disbursed annually. Extracting data-driven insight from this at scale, across systems that were never designed to talk to each other, is the core problem SIP addresses.

Architecturally, SIP is a pipeline: disparate structured and unstructured systems of record are consolidated into a data lakehouse, passed through a data-curation layer, and exposed through a cross-system semantic graph that agents query via MCP, with an agentic chat/workflow layer as the user-facing product. Getting the curation layer right required deep engagement with data owners — not just to clean and deduplicate documents, extract structured fields, and semantically chunk unstructured ones, but to capture the tacit rules (join logic, reporting conventions, historical answer patterns) that make results trustworthy. Governance is treated as newly urgent under AI: PII masking, sensitive-data classification, and per-user entitlements matter more because AI makes previously hard-to-reach data far more accessible.

The graph itself layers several hierarchies over one connected structure. A funding hierarchy models an additive DAG from top-level strategy down through funding teams, portfolios, and investments, with a shortcut path connecting funds directly to budgets since multiple teams can co-fund a single investment; investments in turn link out to organizations and to observable outputs like published reports and products.
