---
title: Data Contracts That Actually Work
speaker: Peter, Audax
video: https://youtu.be/9THtkIZ6Xac
---
The talk opens with a familiar data-engineering pain point: pipelines break constantly, sometimes silently, and the root cause is usually that a schema was treated as the whole contract when it's really just a small slice of the metadata a pipeline depends on. Producers change how a field is calculated, whether it can be null, whether it's unique, or how late data can arrive — and none of that is captured anywhere except in the heads of the people who built the pipeline. Consumers are left at the mercy of upstream changes they never agreed to, with only a schema standing between them and the producer's implicit assumptions.

The proposed fix is to make that implicit knowledge explicit through data contracts — an agreement between producer and consumer that is enforceable, testable, automatable, and shareable, not just descriptive documentation. This is the motivation behind the Open Data Contract Standard (ODCS), a vendor-agnostic open standard now under the Linux Foundation, originally brought out of PayPal by Jean-Georges Perrin and developed since by a volunteer technical steering committee. The core idea is to treat data the way OpenAPI treats HTTP APIs: a shared, tool-agnostic specification that everyone — producers, consumers, business analysts, product owners — can read, validate against, and build tooling around.

An ODCS contract goes well beyond column names and types. It captures constraints (nullability, uniqueness, ranges, custom SQL or data-quality-engine rules), ownership and contact details, versioning and compatibility across contract changes, SLAs for freshness and delivery timing, and security/access rules, alongside logical and physical type mappings so the same contract can describe data sitting in Postgres, Iceberg, or elsewhere. Because all of this metadata lives in one place, the contract becomes a single source of truth from which many downstream artifacts can be generated: alerts, monitoring rules, boilerplate code, tests, documentation, retention policies, and even entries in whatever data catalog a team happens to use (Collibra, DataHub, etc.), with the contract-to-tool mapping acting as a thin translation layer rather than a migration.

Practically, contracts as lightweight YAML files slot naturally into CI/CD: they can be validated automatically, used to generate production-like synthetic test data so pipelines can be exercised without touching real production data, and used to catch schema or rule violations before deployment. On governance, the pitch is that instead of a catalog UI where a governance team clicks buttons and the process stops there, governance rules live in the contract itself and become actionable and portable across whatever catalog or quality tool an organization adopts.

On adoption, the advice is to start small — pick the one production pipeline that causes the most pain, mine the tribal knowledge sitting in emails and analysts' heads, encode it into a contract, and iterate from there rather than attempting a full data-mesh rollout up front. Data contracts aren't presented as mandatory for every team; the value should be judged case by case. The end state being aimed for is a producer and consumer with no hidden assumptions between them, aligned expectations, and far less finger-pointing when something breaks, since the agreed-upon contract itself settles disputes much like a legal contract would.

Audience questions round out the practical picture. Enforcement is two-sided: producers can test that what they emit adheres to the contract, and consumers can validate incoming data against it, with any disagreement treated as something that should have been caught when the contract was written. Tooling already exists to operationalize this — the open-source Data Contract CLI can read an ODCS YAML file and generate SQL, Soda data-quality rules, and other artifacts, with more tools listed on the ODCS GitHub. On alert fatigue, the honest answer is that contracts don't solve it outright, but they do let teams tag data-quality rules by category (conformity, uniqueness, freshness, etc.) so that out of potentially hundreds of rules, only the ones that matter most trigger actionable alerts.
