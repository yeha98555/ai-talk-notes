---
title: How Forward Deployed Engineering is Done at Factory
speaker: Eno Reyes, Factory
video: https://youtu.be/wpOA-UXynoM
---
Opening the forward-deployed engineering track, Eno Reyes starts from the Palantir-era playbook — parachuting strong engineers behind the customer's curtain because integration work could only happen inside their environment — and observes that the role has since forked into ambiguous variants: professional services on the customer's behalf, transforming the product around one customer, or building net-new systems in the customer's environment. Factory draws a hard line against the first: when a customer asks them to execute a codebase modernization that consulting firms have quoted, the answer is no, even if Factory's own product would do the work — services revenue is decent money but doesn't compound into a better product or a massively scalable business.

Instead, deployed engineers are "the tip of the spear of the product": the stream of information from the largest, most critical customers — engineering leadership, on-the-ground tactical engineers, how software development and AI actually happen in that org — flowing back to rapidly reshape the product to fit customer environments. The ambition is that Factory effectively self-assembles inside a customer's environment, which is a necessity, not a luxury, when the customer has 45,000 people, potentially hundreds of thousands of engineers, and tens of thousands of codebases that no one can manually install into.

What Factory sells is described as building blocks for a "software factory," resting on an implicit process every organization already runs: signals flow in from the world — customer conversations, bug reports, internal Slack or Teams threads, an executive decree — humans triage and plan, developers convert plans into changes to a source of truth, changes pass through validation (code review, QA, security assessment, SAST tools, linters, type checkers), and shipped software generates new signals. Most orgs instrument this feedback loop poorly; transform each stage with AI and you can reach a signal-to-deploy flow uninterrupted by a human — which does not mean humans are absent, only that they engineer the system rather than sit inside the loop. This is "built, not bought," and the platform's pitch is the canonical model-independent agent harness for it: Droid gives model independence, full ownership of every trace and datum flowing through, centralized enterprise governance, and can be fully air-gapped — finance, healthcare, and government partners run contained software factories, and one deployed engineer jokes you could run Droid in a submarine.

The deployed engineer's job in that context is threefold: make the product self-assemble into the environment, teach the org a model of the transformation — most organizations have no autonomy maturity model or roadmap for becoming an autonomous software organization — and construct the ROI story from the start, e.g. changes passing AI code review, QA, and security analysis are some 87% less likely to hit a bug, which reduces bug rate, lifts customer satisfaction, and flows to revenue. In this world engineers move from directly manipulating software to maintaining and managing a system that builds software, an abstraction jump that even thoughtful engineers find genuinely hard; the people best suited are DevEx engineers already thinking about enablement, product managers willing to get technical fast, and anyone formed on teams where high-quality dev environments were a priority.

A large share of the work is making codebases agent-ready, which Reyes defines as a measure of how many deterministic validation loops exist in a codebase — the more feedback loops, the longer agents can operate on complex tasks without human intervention. Factory's tooling scans for gaps, and maybe 30–40% of the low-hanging fruit is literally "Droid, please fix all of this"; the other 60% requires workflow changes and sensitivity to humans unused to the nitpickiness of automated validation. The endgame harness is "missions": long-running work with almost no human involvement beyond the planning stage — define a bounded task and what solving it means, then "push a lever of inference until the task is complete." Anywhere "complete" is verifiable this is unbelievably competent: fully autonomous migrations of 30–50-million-plus-line codebases, deep-learning work in biomed, and equity-research systems at financial institutions. The training parallel makes it unsurprising — models are post-trained on dense reward, and an org's verification signals are exactly the reward that keeps a long-horizon agent on track. Hence the deployed engineer's counterintuitive advice: invest less in solving the problem, more in preparing the environment to verify the problem.

For scaling beyond the first deployment, Reyes reaches for Walt Disney's EPCOT: a working exemplar of the "city of the future" gets copied by other cities — but overbuild it and it becomes a theme park nobody believes applies to their world. The future is unevenly distributed today: Factory's own codebase runs with an autonomy ratio in the upper 80s, some customers' more constrained codebases are even more autonomous, and contained internal tools get to 100% first — Factory's "legal droid" workflow is already fully autonomously maintained, while the core harness is not, because hard visual problems like terminal flickering still lack verifiable checks. Building verifiers for what only humans can currently see is, he suggests, a picture of where the human work goes next. The closing pitch: former founders (each SDLC stage Droid covers — code review, incident response, QA, testing — is a billion-dollar business), fluent technical communicators with executive presence, and systems thinkers who love closing loops are the people who thrive in the role.
