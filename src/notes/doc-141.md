---
title: Evaling Video Slop
speaker: Maor Bril, Character.ai
video: https://youtu.be/b_PmGocP4rc
---
The talk tackles a gap in the current video-generation stack: generation quality has raced ahead (Kling, SeaDance, VEO, Sora) while evaluation has lagged behind, still relying on humans squinting at outputs or on tools borrowed from the text and image eras. Metrics like CLIP score judge a single frame against a prompt, and LPIPS catches frame-to-frame drift, but neither tells you whether the video actually told the story it set out to tell — whether physics holds, characters stay consistent across shots, pacing feels natural, and audio (a door slamming, footsteps) is synced to the imagery.

The first iteration replaced ad hoc human judgment with a repeatable benchmark harness: classic frame-level metrics combined with an LLM-as-judge, calibrated against human annotations fed back into the judge's prompt. This produced consistent, reviewable reports, but it was slow and expensive — a real problem when the goal is to catch errors as early and as cheaply as possible. Catching a drifted starting frame before it's used to generate a full shot, or catching a bad six-second clip before it's stitched into a multi-minute video, is far cheaper than fixing the assembled result.

To close that gap, the team distilled the "committee of experts" (metrics plus LLM judges) into a single small vision-language model, built on Qwen, that doesn't just score a video but explains why it scored poorly — extra limb, broken physics, unsynced audio — and does so fast (about 3 seconds for a 15-second clip). A larger model scored better but was too slow to justify the gain. A second key finding was to train on relative comparisons rather than absolute scores: people disagree wildly on whether a video is a 6 or an 8, but largely agree on which of two videos tells the better story, so the model was trained on pairwise data, with "bad" examples manufactured by corrupting good videos or generating slop outright.

The first version of this judge model was confidently wrong — rating a static, unmoving shot 9.2 on cinematography, or praising the physics in a clip of a hovering ghost — because it had learned to score general "vibe" and surface polish rather than the specific axes (story, physics, pacing) it was meant to judge. The fix was to pair real footage against AI-generated footage rather than good AI clips against bad ones, while keeping encoding and annotation methodology identical across both sides to avoid training an AI-content detector instead of a quality detector. That data fix produced much better results.

The system then moved from a fixed evaluation pipeline to an agentic workflow, because users bring highly distinct stories, characters, and voices that a rigid pipeline can't anticipate; giving an agent tools to validate its own output lets it adapt and self-correct as it goes. Sound evaluation layers audio-quality checks (Dolby Atmos) with frame-level correlation — the model looks for a spike in the audio track at the same timestamp as, say, a door slamming, without necessarily identifying the specific sound. Lip-syncing remains unsolved, especially for stylized talking-head animations with no real anatomical correlation to speech. Human taste is folded in continuously: periodic annotation sessions across random axes calibrate the LLM judges and feed a growing dataset for the next model version, an admittedly slow and inherently subjective process.

On the question of when this approach makes sense: model and serving scale is a unit-economics decision, not a technical requirement — a slower cohort of open metrics and frontier judges works fine at low volume, but a fast, cheap distilled judge pays off once you're generating thousands of videos a day. The closing recommendations: evaluate relatively, not absolutely; score the specific axes you actually care about rather than expecting them to emerge from a generic quality score; push evaluation as close to the generation loop as possible; and ultimately judge video as a story, since video is just another storytelling medium.
