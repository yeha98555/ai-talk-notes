---
title: Thinner Agents on a Smarter Substrate: The Ontology-based Semantic Layer
speaker: Emil Eifrem, Neo4j
video: https://youtu.be/VGN22pPpb-8
---
The talk starts from a concrete enterprise scenario: an agent that automates opening a bank account. Stripped down, any such agent has two parts — the business logic that interprets intent and plans/acts in a loop, and the data sources it must reach into to do real work, such as a DMV registry and a passport-verification service to validate identity. Building one such agent this way works fine. The problem shows up when many teams across a large organization build many agents that all look structurally similar.

Four problems recur at scale. First, every team has to rediscover from scratch where the needed data lives — trivial with one Postgres database, brutal across a hundred databases, Snowflake, Databricks, and S3 buckets. Second, once data sources are found, teams still have to judge which copy is authoritative, trustworthy, and permissioned, since enterprise data is heavily duplicated. Third, this wiring violates DRY: business intent and data-source logic get baked into ad hoc code and prompts per agent, so any change cascades into manual rework everywhere. Fourth, there is no learning — an agent that wakes up tomorrow is no smarter than today, and there is no way for one agent's experience to benefit another.

Markdown-based "skills" files are addressed directly as a partial but insufficient answer — useful, but, as quoted from a recent Latent Space conversation, you still have to actually know your databases rather than vibe-coding against files alone. Having solved this problem at scale for organizations including a Fortune 20 bank, a large Bay Area tech platform, and a leading fintech, the pattern that emerges is "thin agents on a smarter shared substrate."

That substrate rests on three pillars. A business-facing ontology captures the core concepts of the organization — customers, accounts, debit cards, checks, transactions, and their relationships — expressed in the vocabulary people actually use (a customer has a first name, not an f_name field). A technical ontology captures the metadata of every real data source and asset in the ecosystem: which Oracle and Neo4j databases exist, what's in Snowflake and Databricks, what schemas look like, and where everything sits. A mapping layer then binds the two together, so a business concept like "first name" resolves to its system of record and the specific column that holds it. On top of these, a third layer of runtime signals captures execution traces from agents as they actually walk this graph — what they tried, whether it succeeded, and what the outcome was.

In the account-opening example, the ontology encodes not just concepts like checks, accounts, and credit history but also the business process itself, since this is meant to be a process-guided agent. A node like "check compliance" links into the technical ontology, which specifies that resolving a government-issued ID can be done via DMV records or passport verification. As agents execute against this graph, they leave execution traces recording which path they used and whether it worked, and that feedback becomes input for future runs — an agent that has had more success with the DMV lookup becomes more likely to choose it again in similar contexts, producing continual, cross-agent learning rather than static wiring.

Together, the three pillars address all four original problems: data sources become discoverable and their trustworthiness is knowable both top-down (via human-curated ontology) and bottom-up (via execution traces); business intent and data sources are mapped once in a governed, shared place instead of being duplicated per agent; and the system exhibits genuine self-learning, both within a single agent over time and across the fleet of agents. The result is a shift from thick, manually wired agents toward thin agents sitting on top of a shared, ontology-based semantic layer, which makes it practical to build and scale many agents without re-engineering each one from scratch.
