---
title: Forward Deployed Engineering 101
speaker: Kevin Bai, Anthropic
video: https://youtu.be/KwhgfwOSToQ
---
This talk is a primer on forward deployed engineering (FDE) — the go-to-market model Palantir pioneered and that the speaker later helped scale at Rippling before joining Anthropic — tracing where the role comes from, why it exists, and when it makes sense to adopt.

Palantir's core product, Foundry, centralizes an organization's data into an "ontology" (proper nouns instead of scattered tables) and lets customers build applications on top. Sold as pure technology, this falls flat: buyers care about outcomes like shelf placement or sales throughput, not data architecture, and a platform's success depends entirely on how well customers can use it — a heavy training tax on top of the purchase price. The fix was to stop selling software or services separately and instead sell an outcome: smart engineers embedded with the customer, understanding their business and building solutions on the platform, so the customer buys a result rather than a tool or a headcount.

The need for FDE is framed as a two-by-two: technical platforms sold to technical buyers (GitHub, Datadog) don't need it, because CTOs and engineers can absorb the complexity themselves; simple, configurable products sold to non-technical buyers (Rippling, Jira, Slack) don't need it either. FDE only becomes necessary in the awkward quadrant where a highly technical, app-building platform must be sold to a non-technical buyer — Palantir's exact position, since large tech companies with strong internal engineering (Google, Meta) don't need this help, but a Fortune 500 oil-and-gas company does. Rather than hoping the customer invests the time to get value from the platform, Palantir loans them engineers who already know the platform and work closely with the client to build the solution, like attentive service at a fine-dining restaurant. The results are cited as evidence this works: among public SaaS companies, Palantir's average contract value dwarfs peers like ServiceNow and Workday, achieved with only a few thousand employees.

FDE is explicitly framed as scaling the startup practice of design partnerships — deeply understanding one customer's problem and building for them — into the enterprise, at scale, indefinitely rather than just during the early product-market-fit phase. The critical caveat, though, is that FDEs must build on top of a shared platform with reusable primitives, never from scratch; an FDE org where every engineer writes bespoke code per customer isn't an FDE function at all, it's a dev shop — profitable perhaps, but doomed to a maintenance nightmare and engineer burnout without the leverage of shared building blocks.

For anyone considering this model, two gating questions are offered. First, do you actually need it — is there a genuine, unavoidable situation where a technically complex product must be sold to a non-technical buyer? If not, better options exist: developer relations for technical go-to-market, or sales-led growth for traditional SaaS. Second, do you have (or are you willing to build) a real platform with shared primitives that FDEs assemble from, rather than reinvent each time?

On why this matters now: AI has made it dramatically easier to write code and build customizable software, so agentic platforms are proliferating across every vertical — insurance, legal, and beyond. The speaker's hypothesis is not that everyone has suddenly recognized FDE as clever, but that the nature of software itself has shifted: because virtually every platform is now agentic and therefore customizable, virtually every vendor now faces customers who don't understand what the product actually does — pushing more companies toward exactly the technical-platform/non-technical-buyer quadrant that originally necessitated FDE.

Audience questions add texture: shared primitives should be as atomic as the use case demands — sometimes an app can be 60% pre-built platform and 40% customization, other times (as with AWS's DynamoDB-style building blocks) far more granular tooling is needed depending on how broad the customer base is. Bespoke work for one customer should stay bespoke; anything generalizable should eventually be folded back into the platform, and early FDE engagements are themselves useful for scouting which capabilities deserve to become core product. Multiple FDEs (or firms) can and should collaborate on the same account to avoid single points of failure. And the ideal FDE profile is simply "a customer-facing software engineer" — someone you'd hire onto an engineering team but also trust in front of a client.
