This is the third installment in Hugging Face's live-stream series on training agents, following sessions on SFT-on-traces and distillation, and setting up a fourth session that will pair the same algorithm with real RL environments. The throughline across all three is the density of the learning signal: SFT gives a dense, per-token target to emulate but caps out at an "emulation ceiling" defined by the training data; on-policy distillation gives dense, per-token feedback from a teacher on the student's own fresh rollouts, which is richer but still bounded by the teacher's knowledge; reinforcement learning gives only one sparse signal — a reward at the end of a trajectory — but that signal is genuinely on-policy, letting the model learn from its own attempts rather than someone else's. In practice pipelines chain these together: SFT bootstraps a model up to a usable baseline (like reading grandmaster games), and RL then pushes it further through its own trial and error (like actually playing tournaments).

The algorithm covered is GRPO (group relative policy optimization). One GRPO step takes a prompt, samples a group of multiple completions from the current policy (not just one), scores each completion with a reward function (typically plain Python — checking things like a legal chess move, task success, or a passing test suite), and converts those raw rewards into an advantage per completion: reward minus the group's mean, divided by the group's standard deviation. That group-relative advantage, not the absolute reward, is what drives the policy update, which is why the composition and variation within a group matters so much: if every completion in a group succeeds or every one fails, there is no relative signal to learn from. Two guardrails keep the policy from drifting too far while it optimizes this signal: a KL-divergence penalty (parameter beta) that makes it costlier for the policy to move away from its starting behavior, and clipping (parameter epsilon) that bounds how far any single batch's update can shift the policy. Both default to zero in TRL and are meant to be tuned deliberately.

The reward function is described as a contract that defines exactly what "success" means, and it's the real coalface of RL — most of the iteration effort in a project goes into getting reward functions right rather than the training loop itself. A common pattern stacks a "format" reward (was the output structured correctly, e.g. a legal move or well-tagged answer) with an "accuracy" reward (was the underlying answer or task actually correct), since a model can be correctly formatted but wrong, or right but mis-formatted, and each combination should score differently. Reward shaping this way (giving partial credit for format even before the harder accuracy signal is learnable) helps create the variation a group needs, and it's also central to avoiding a task that's simply too hard (no signal, like an amateur playing grandmasters every game) or too easy (no signal, like a grandmaster playing amateurs). DeepSeek's papers' "aha moment" is cited as the point where enough variation accumulates for learning to visibly take off.

Reward hacking — a model finding a shortcut that raises reward without actually solving the task — is treated as close to inevitable if a reward function has any exploitable gap (an open-ended regex, an ambiguous win condition). KL penalty makes hacking somewhat costlier because a hack is usually a behavior far from the model's initial (SFT) distribution, but it's not a real fix, since it's also suppressing other useful novel behavior. The actual defense is watching metrics (falling entropy while reward keeps climbing is a red flag) and constantly reading the underlying trajectories and data rather than just admiring the reward curve, plus holding out a separate test-reward signal that should track the training reward — a widening gap between train and test reward is the clearest tell.

GRPO is contrasted with DPO, an offline method trained on fixed chosen/rejected pairs against a frozen reference model — simpler to set up, but stale data can leave little signal beyond what the policy already knows — and with online DPO as a middle ground. PPO, the algorithm behind the original InstructGPT-style RLHF, is more complex still, requiring a separate learned reward model to grade each generation, which adds cost but can capture stylistic qualities that a deterministic reward function can't; many of its ideas persist across later RL-for-LLM methods even though it's rarely the first choice today.

Mapped onto TRL's GRPOConfig, the loop's knobs are: temperature and num_generations (group size) at sampling time, max completion length and whether to use vLLM for inference; reward scaling across multiple reward functions (e.g. down-weighting format relative to accuracy so the model doesn't overfit to formatting); epsilon/epsilon_high and loss type for the clipped token loss; and beta plus learning rate for the policy update, alongside steps-per-generation and num_iterations controlling how "online" training is. For reading Trackio curves: an easy reward (like format) should rise quickly, a harder reward (like task success) should rise more slowly but steadily, entropy should stay roughly flat-to-declining, and reward spread should show healthy variation rather than flatlining. A stalled run shows no reward signal and no spread at all; a collapsing run shows entropy dropping sharply, completion length spiking, one reward hacked upward, and KL/beta climbing as the policy drifts from its starting point.

Sergio ran three escalating experiments on Hugging Face Jobs (no local GPU needed), all logged to Trackio. The first used a deliberately dummy reward — rewarding outputs close to 20 characters — purely to show that GRPO will optimize exactly what you tell it to, reward climbing even though the objective is meaningless. The second trained a small (~0.6B) coding model on a dataset of Python problems with unit tests, using the fraction of passing tests (executed in a sandboxed, timeout-bound process) as a verifiable reward; a baseline check showed the untrained model averaging only 0.22 reward across generations, and training was compared across group sizes of 2, 8, and 16 generations to find the sweet spot where rewards are neither all-zero nor all-one. The third experiment used an explicitly gameable reward — rewarding the model simply for emitting four Python code blocks, regardless of correctness — and showed the classic reward-hacking signature: training reward climbing to its ceiling while held-out test reward stayed flat, exposing the gap between optimizing the metric and solving the task.

Practical recommendations from the Q&A: dry-run a reward function on sample text before any training; then smoke-test with a handful of steps and generations to confirm the reward shows any variation at all before committing to a full run; dataset sizes for meaningful capability shifts can be as small as a few thousand samples, though published work often uses far more; and diagnosing generalization versus overfitting or hacking requires looking at multiple curves and hyperparameters together (group size, temperature, reward composition) rather than any single number. The next session extends this same GRPO loop into full reinforcement-learning environments where the "world" the agent acts in, not just the reward function, has to be built.
