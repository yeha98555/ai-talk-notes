---
title: How Evals and Prompts Shape Agent Behavior
speaker: Preetika Bhateja & Daniel Bump, YouTube Ads
video: https://youtu.be/xyL2Ltkh-SA
---
The talk comes from the YouTube Ads team, which builds image and video generation agents for ad creatives, and it lays out a practical playbook for building evals that actually make agents reliable rather than merely feeling reliable. The starting premise is that agent reliability is a function of three things — the underlying capabilities, the guardrails, and the evals — and that evals only work well once the foundation is solid: a focused, LLM-friendly toolset should be optimized first, and an independent critique agent with a remediation loop can catch gaps the base tools miss, before a team invests heavily in a comprehensive eval suite.

A counterintuitive early recommendation is that "vibing" — informal, non-scalable, intuition-driven review of outputs — is actually valuable at the start. It lets a small team quickly see failure patterns and make radical prompt or architecture changes without a rigid eval holding them back, building the depth of understanding that later informs a real eval design. Jumping straight to scaled raters too early instead produces noisy, unstable quality swings, since the team ends up calibrating the rubric and the model at the same time.

Evals should start small: pick a handful of core tasks the agent must nail, and test negatives — checking that the agent does *not* do a bad thing — as rigorously as checking that it completes the task, since writing the eval is often quick but agreeing on the rubric among humans is the hard part. As the effort scales up to cross-functional teams and outside raters, a few practices proved important: give raters an explicit rubric with concrete examples to minimize ambiguous edge cases and disagreement; and always collect explanations alongside pass/fail judgments, since a bare verdict says nothing about *why* something failed or which dimension (e.g., accuracy vs. brand safety) is weak in multi-output, multi-turn evaluations.

For LLM-as-judge setups, the team monitored agreement rates between LLM judges and human/expert raters to track calibration over time, and pushed beyond pass/fail into inspecting the agent's actual reasoning traces. A concrete failure illustrates why: the agent was repeatedly instructed that legal disclaimers must never be removed from an ad, yet a pass/fail metric alone couldn't catch cases where it still removed one. Only by reading the trace did the team see the agent explicitly reason "I found a disclaimer, now I'll remove it" — a failure invisible without trace-level inspection.

Classic ML practices still apply: agents don't generalize well beyond their training and test distribution, so a held-out test set for edge cases and broader capability checks remains valuable, used sparingly and periodically refreshed with production data to avoid overfitting to it.

Hill-climbing on quality is an iterative loop: run human eval, and if it doesn't meet the bar, dig into precision/recall on the eval set, then adjust either the rubric, the model, or the agent's tooling, and repeat. For launch readiness, teams need to identify where and why performance regresses and distinguish acceptable trade-offs from critical failures — and, crucially, resist over-indexing on any single failing example, since these are non-deterministic systems; what matters is the frequency of a failure pattern across a representative golden set, not one bad run. Investing in online evals that track real-world data distribution is equally important.

The closing synthesis: a good eval system should represent what the product actually needs to excel at, which changes as the agent moves from MVP to production. It must keep evolving through online evals, refreshed test sets, and sampling pipelines; rely on highly curated, continuously updated golden sets; train raters and cross-functional teams explicitly on rating expectations; supply clear rater templates and rubrics with examples to avoid ambiguous "unknown" judgments; and settle early on concrete launch/gatekeeping metrics, since the right bar (precision/recall or otherwise) differs by use case.
