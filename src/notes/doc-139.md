---
title: Architecting Modern AI Systems: Platforms, Agents, and Integration
speaker: Frédéric (MIA), Shao (Bell Canada), and Allan (BUZZ HPC) — panel discussion
video: https://youtu.be/kDAlW3vRQzI
---
This panel of three practitioners — from a Canadian applied-AI research group, Bell Canada, and the sovereign GPU cloud provider BUZZ HPC — uses a mental-health hackathon they just ran together (with Bell and Kids Help Phone) as a jumping-off point to talk about how modern agentic systems actually get built: what a platform should own versus what a team must own, and where the gap between hackathon demo and production really lives. The hackathon itself — over 100 teams building guardrails to decide when a conversational agent should escalate to a human, evaluated through a self-service leaderboard the panelists built on Kubernetes over BUZZ's HPC infrastructure — drew roughly a thousand submissions in a week, and a mid-week hidden dataset was introduced specifically to catch teams overfitting to the visible leaderboard.

A recurring theme is tokenomics and control: it's trivial to get an API key from a frontier lab and prototype quickly, but teams that need scale, data residency, or predictable behavior eventually want to host open-source models themselves. BUZZ frames itself as the infrastructure that removes the skills barrier to doing this — provisioning GPUs, running inference engines (vLLM, SGLang, TensorRT-LLM), scaling to zero and back, and letting any coding-agent harness (Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, Open Code) point at an open model instead of a closed one. Concrete motivations include avoiding the cost structure of zero-data-retention arrangements — burning through a quota five minutes in and coming back to pay 10× the token cost — and reclaiming control over output diversity and creativity through research-grade techniques like LoRA adapters, steering vectors, and advanced sampling algorithms that closed APIs never expose.

Hardware economics get their own segment: there's a real tradeoff between GPU generation (Blackwell's efficiency on FP4-quantized models) and GPU capacity (an A100's 80 GB of VRAM still fits models some Blackwell inference cards can't), and rents have spiked with the supply crunch — A100s bottomed around $1.50/hour mid-last-year and are now hard to find under $2. The working heuristic: use the strongest model for tasks you're not sure can be done at all, or where you truly need gold-standard output, and smaller models for routine grunt work. Locally-runnable 27–35B models are visibly limited, but 200B-to-trillion-parameter open models on high-end GPUs now compete with closed flagships on flexibility and cost.

Another pain point with closed APIs is loss of control: providers can quietly swap in quantized or degraded variants without telling anyone, or upgrade a model out from under you and break every prompt and test you've tuned. A self-hosted, version-pinned open model buys predictability and reproducibility instead.

The hackathon-to-production gap is framed as "80% is fast, 95% is hard": prototyping is easy, but production quality demands real evaluation, iteration, and user feedback. Over-relying on LLM-as-judge just pushes the problem back a level — who judges the judge? — so human evals grounded in the actual business need (product-manager thinking), sometimes even high-grade human annotation to build gold-standard data combined with RLHF-style customization, remain necessary. RL gyms, verifiable rewards (Lean proof-checkers paired with LLMs are now cracking Erdős problems), and world models come up as the next frontier for training more robust agents.

Each panelist sketches their agentic stack. Bell's approach is three-tiered: an enterprise-wide general LLM / light-RAG platform for non-technical staff; switching on the agentic features already built into purchased enterprise software (the lowest-friction, highest-ROI move, matching the current mood of exhausting already-paid-for tools before buying yet another SaaS); and custom cloud production systems for specific business needs. Frédéric stresses how fast the stack has churned — prompt engineering to RAG to tools, memory, MCP, harnesses — and deliberately avoids marrying any of it: deliver value today, but know your exit path. Allan's gap is constrained/structured generation — grammar-constrained decoding that forces output formats or tool-call order; closed providers withhold it for safety and alignment reasons (prefix injection can bypass safety training), while BUZZ exposes it to customers it trusts, and this hybrid of deterministic flow plus LLM free rein is exactly what lets deep-research-style agents dramatically outperform default coding agents.

Governance and observability are treated as table stakes for enterprise deployment: an agent deserves at least the level of oversight (if not the identical kind) a human employee gets; its reachable resources and databases should be scoped, and MCP tools wrapped so irreversible operations are blocked. The industry already has real incidents — agents deleting databases, or firing tens of thousands of requests at Jira — which makes process discipline as important as the tooling itself.

For validating agent output, the panel suggests building QA-agent skills — screenshot a PR's UI, click through it, screenshot again, and emit a pass/fail report — plus cloud simulation environments for pre-merge testing. But the foundation is still classic software engineering: define requirements and architecture up front, have agents review each other and self-critique, and close the loop between generated code and its actual visual/UX result, since LLMs read text-shaped code fluently but don't necessarily intuit what a pile of CSS will render as. On the observability side, OpenTelemetry-based tools like AgentOps and Arize Phoenix record tool calls and MCP traces, but once logs reach hundreds of millions of tokens, human or LLM-assisted triage gets expensive — filter with non-AI heuristics first, and put the emphasis on preventing failures (e.g., blocking irreversible actions) rather than merely detecting them.

A closing caution: sandboxes matter but are not a hard boundary — as models get stronger at code generation, practitioners widely report sandboxes are brittle, and even Nvidia's agent projects are, to a large degree, "better sandboxes" — so treat sandboxing as delaying escape rather than ruling it out. BUZZ also shares its current GPU rates (H100s about $2.50/hour, H200s $3.50, A40s $0.50, no A100s in stock; long-term or volume commitments negotiable, Blackwell by inquiry), echoing the earlier discussion of hardware costs climbing fast.
