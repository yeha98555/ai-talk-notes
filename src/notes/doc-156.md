---
title: Rethinking Environments for Long-Horizon Work
speaker: Rayan Garg, Theta Software
video: https://youtu.be/2aS7aKoXn64
---
The talk opens by interrogating a term that gets thrown around loosely: "long horizon." Rather than a binary label, horizon length is a scalar, and what counted as long a year ago won't in a year or two. Two reference frames are used to measure it. The human-horizon view, popularized by METR, benchmarks tasks against how long they take a human at a given success threshold (e.g., a 50% success rate on tasks that take a human 16 hours). The model-native view instead counts tokens, steps, and tool calls consumed in a trajectory — noisy, since harness and model choice swing the numbers a lot, but still informative about how hard a task is for an agent to stay coherent across, especially once compaction and context limits come into play.

Both views have blind spots. Tasks that are tedious for humans (reformatting a giant spreadsheet) can be trivial for a model that just writes a script, while tasks trivial for humans can be hard for models. Methodology also varies wildly across teams reporting "16-hour" or "20-hour" benchmarks, since expert quality and measurement protocol aren't standardized — and this noise only grows as tasks approach the frontier of human expertise. The two lenses diverge because human and agent limitations differ, so neither alone gives the full picture.

To actually measure model capability, the talk proposes three axes of environment complexity beyond raw duration. First, tool coordination: how many external systems and dependencies (Grafana, GitHub CI/CD, CloudWatch, databases) an agent must move information across. Second, state change: whether earlier decisions meaningfully affect later ones. Chaining unrelated subtasks together to inflate duration doesn't measure real capability; what matters is whether the environment's state cascades — parallelizable work like scanning many files with sub-agents involves little state coupling, while sequential work like querying dashboards means an early mistake compounds downstream. Third, ambiguity: how much information the task gives up front. Mirroring how humans actually work — incomplete information, exploration required — makes evaluation more realistic but also harder to standardize, since many different paths can be valid.

The hardest piece of building these environments is the verifier. As tasks move from cleanly verifiable domains (math, data-structure coding) into open-ended, real-world "software-fellow" domains, deterministic checks (unit tests, proofs) become impractical or impossible, pushing teams toward judge/critic models that assess both the final state of the environment and the trajectory taken to reach it. Judges matter because not all correct-looking solutions are equally valid — reward hacking (escaping a sandbox, peeking at a hidden test suite) can only be caught by scrutinizing process, not just outcome. Over-constraining trajectories to match a reference solution collapses the space of valid paths and breaks down on ambiguous tasks with many legitimate solutions.

Several design principles follow. Judges are themselves agents: they need harness support matching the task environment (e.g., read access to GitHub and CloudWatch logs to confirm a deployment actually succeeded), with safeguards like enforced read-only access so the judge can't mutate state. Trajectories must be made queryable rather than dumped whole into a judge's context — parsed into phases, enriched with metadata, and searchable so failure points can be located in long, complex runs. Learnability depends on reward density and rubric quality: overly dense rubrics on frontier-hard problems cause judges to apply them inconsistently, so rubric QA (gold answers, no-op checks, coverage, expert agreement) becomes essential, alongside patterns like combining deterministic sub-checks with judges or using dynamic, partial-credit rubrics that account for earlier agent assumptions.

The talk closes by applying this framework to critique existing finance benchmarks (GDPval, ToolBench, Apex agents): their average human-hours-per-task fall well below genuine long-horizon thresholds, they're already near-saturated, their task coverage is narrow (e.g., mostly investment-banking or Excel tasks, missing credit, debt, and risk), and their reward signals are far less granular than the fine-grained sub-criteria needed to actually train models. By contrast, Theta's own finance-domain data averages 15 human-hours per task across a 50-task sample, and models still struggle significantly across the domains tested — illustrating why rigorous, complexity-aware environment and verifier design matters for making long-horizon evaluation and training meaningful.
